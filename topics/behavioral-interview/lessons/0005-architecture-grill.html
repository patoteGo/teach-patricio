"The project you're most proud of —
architecturally."

Yesterday this question slipped away from you. Here's the diagnosis: it is not a story question — it's an architecture-judgment question wearing a story costume. The interviewer isn't scoring your narrative. They're asking: does this person understand what they built, and can they justify every decision in it? This lesson is a grill deck for the Cencosud ingest project: one upgraded pitch, then the follow-up probes on SQS, data, scale, and team that a senior interviewer will throw.

1Why the answer didn't convince

"Project you're proud of in an architectural sense" has a hidden rubric. The interviewer is scoring four things, and a straight STAR pitch only covers the first:

1 · You shipped something real

Scale, ownership, impact. Your BLUF already does this — millions of events/day, spikes, one source of truth. This part you nailed yesterday.

2 · Decision justification

Why SQS, why Node, why batch inserts, why normalize at the edge. Each decision needs a because — and a rejected alternative named. "We used SQS" scores zero; "SQS over Kafka because events-per-second didn't justify operating Kafka" scores senior.

3 · Depth of mechanics

Do you know what you actually operated? At-least-once delivery, visibility timeout, consumer lag, DLQ behavior. This is where "I built it" separates from "I was on the team."

4 · Team- and org-level judgment

Architecture is a people decision too: Java estate + one Node service, who owned the event contract, what you'd do differently. Senior = you designed for the organization, not just the workload.

The one-line fix: after your BLUF, immediately name your three architectural decisions — "(1) SQS as the decoupling backbone, (2) normalize at the edge into one internal event, (3) batch writes to Postgres." That sentence is an invitation: the follow-ups now land on ground you chose.

2The pitch (architecture edition, ~45s)

Rehearse this out loud with the timer. Hit every checklist item. Don't memorize the words — memorize the shape.

"The ingest service at Cencosud. We had many external delivery partners, each shipping tracking events in their own format, and every internal service needed one consistent answer to where is this package. I designed and owned the Node service that solved it: SQS in front to decouple partners from consumers and absorb our sale-day spikes, normalization at the edge so every partner format collapsed into one internal tracking event — the contract the whole company consumed — and batch writes into Postgres so the database survived millions of events a day. The architecture decision I'm proudest of isn't any single piece: it's that the whole thing was right-sized — SQS instead of a heavier broker, Node instead of the team's Java default — for the actual workload and the actual team."

4Primary sources

Every model answer above is grounded in the AWS documentation — read the first one tonight, it's short and it's the probe interviewers love most:

  • Amazon SQS at-least-once delivery — the official statement that duplicates happen, i.e. why your idempotency answer exists.
  • SQS visibility timeout — the mechanism behind "what if a consumer dies mid-processing?"
  • SQS dead-letter queues — poison-message handling and maxReceiveCount.
  • SQS message quotas — the 10-message receive batch limit behind your batch-size answer.
  • FIFO exactly-once processing — the tradeoff you name when asked "why not FIFO?"

Stuck mid-probe? Ask your teacher.

This lesson is a mirror, not a wall. If any model answer feels wrong for your system — batch size, lag metric, partner count — open the agent and pressure-test it: "here's what actually happened, help me say it senior." That conversation is the real feedback loop.
