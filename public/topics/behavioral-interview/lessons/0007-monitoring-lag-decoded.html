"How did you know it
was falling behind?"

The observability probe from the Grill. The senior answer is one metric: consumer lag — the age of the oldest message in the queue. This lesson shows why lag is the answer: every signal on a queue-based system, every knob that moves it, and a live simulator of the day p95 latency looked perfectly green while the backlog grew to minutes.

3The trap, live: green latency, red lag

This is the sale-day incident as a simulation. Events arrive faster than the pods can drain (throughput in > out) — yet the per-event p95 latency barely moves, because each event is still processed quickly. Only the queue depth and oldest-message age show the truth. Push the buttons and watch:

Default: 120 in vs 2 × 80 = 160 out — comfortable. Drag inflow past 160 (or hit 🔥) and watch depth/age climb while p95 stays green. Then tick autoscale on lag and let it self-heal — that's the structural fix from the incident retro.

What the sim shows

Lag is the integral of the in/out imbalance: it grows slowly, silently, and long after p95 stops moving. A backlog is invisible to per-event latency — each event is fast; there are just too many of them waiting.

What a CPU dashboard would show

Almost nothing. The Node consumer is I/O-bound — waiting on SQS and Postgres — so CPU sits near-idle while the mountain grows. That's why the sale-day scaling signal was lag, not CPU, and why the retro action was HPA-on-lag.

4The knobs — and which way is "worse"

Monitoring tells you that you're behind; knobs decide how fast you can drain. Interviewers ask about one of these to check you actually tuned the system. Click a setting to see its consequence:

How to say it: "Each knob was tuned by the same loop — watch lag and commit-time in New Relic, move one knob, watch again." A number with a reasoning loop beats a number with no story.

530-second interview version

"Queue depth and consumer lag — the age of the oldest message — in New Relic, plus throughput in versus out, error rate, and p95 on the consumer. Lag was the one that mattered: it's the leading indicator for a queue-based system — per-event latency can look perfectly healthy while you're silently minutes behind. We translated it for the business: lag is how stale the tracking view is. Alarms on lag and on DLQ depth — dead-letter rate per partner, so a malformed payload had a blast radius of one. And after the sale-day incident we scaled pods on lag, not CPU — an I/O-bound consumer's CPU looks idle even with a mountain of backlog."
